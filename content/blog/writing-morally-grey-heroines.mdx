When I started writing Elowen, I knew she would make readers uncomfortable. She was calculating in a way I was not accustomed to seeing in romantic heroines. She withheld information from people who trusted her. She chose her own survival, more than once, over someone else's comfort.

Early readers asked me if I intended to "redeem" her.

I found the question fascinating, because it assumed she needed redemption — that the things she had done placed her outside the category of characters we are allowed to love.

I disagreed then. I disagree more now.

## What We Mean When We Say "Likeable"

The pressure on female characters to be likeable is not new, but I think it is worth naming. *Likeable* often means: understandable in her mistakes, quick to apologise, self-sacrificing, warm. A character who is cold, self-serving, or unapologetic triggers a specific kind of reader discomfort that is applied far less frequently to male protagonists in the same genre.

I am not interested in writing characters who are liked. I am interested in writing characters who are *felt*.

Elowen is felt. She is morally compromised in ways I am very deliberate about. And she is, in my estimation, more honest about survival and power and desire than a character who always does the right thing.

## The Romance Complication

Here is where it gets interesting: morally grey heroines are particularly charged in romance, because the love interest becomes a kind of test.

Does she deserve him? Does she earn the ending?

I reject the framing — but I also find it useful, because it reveals what the reader actually wants. They want to see her become capable of love. They want the relationship to cost her something real. They want proof that the armour can come off.

That is not redemption. That is intimacy. There is a difference.

## What Grey Actually Looks Like

A morally grey heroine is not cruel without reason. She is not mean to be "edgy." She has a logic — her own code, forged from specific experiences — and she operates by it consistently, even when it conflicts with what is easy or kind.

The craft challenge is making that logic *legible* without excusing everything it produces. The reader has to understand how she got here, while still being allowed to wince when she goes too far.

That tension — between comprehension and discomfort — is where character lives.

Give me women who are difficult. Give me heroines who make choices I disagree with and then make me think about why. Give me the grey.

The love story is always better for it.
