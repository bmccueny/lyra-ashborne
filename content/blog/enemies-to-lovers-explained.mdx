There is a moment in every enemies-to-lovers story that I think of as *the crack* — the instant when the armour shifts just enough to let a sliver of light through. The reader sees it before the characters do. The characters would deny it furiously if you pointed it out. That gap between what is felt and what is admitted is where the entire genre lives.

I have written it three times now, and I will almost certainly write it again.

## Why the Tension Works

The enemies-to-lovers trope works because it is fundamentally a story about **vulnerability**. When we dislike someone, we construct elaborate defences. We catalogue their faults. We rehearse arguments in the shower. We tell everyone who will listen exactly why this person is impossible.

And then, slowly, the defences become the intimacy. Every bitter glance is attention. Every argument is a conversation only the two of them are having.

> "Hatred is just love with nowhere to go." — I have no idea who said this first, but I have thought about it constantly while writing *A Crown of Starless Night*.

## What I Get Wrong (and Right)

In my first draft of *Crown*, Serafine hated Dorian because he had *wronged her*. Which is correct, narratively. But the hate was too clean, too righteous. It took three rewrites to understand: the best enemies-to-lovers tension comes when both characters have a point. When neither one is entirely the villain. When you, as the reader, can see exactly how they ended up here.

The moment I stopped making Serafine right and Dorian wrong — and started making them *both complicated* — the story finally breathed.

## Reading Recommendations

If you want to understand what this trope can do at its absolute peak, I recommend:

- **The Cruel Prince** by Holly Black — the original standard, in my opinion
- **A Court of Thorns and Roses** by Sarah J. Maas — polarising, but the tension in book one is real
- **Bride** by Ali Hazelwood — contemporary, but the bones are the same
