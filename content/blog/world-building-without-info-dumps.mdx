The first draft of *A Crown of Starless Night* opened with four pages explaining the Veilborn magic system.

I thought I was being helpful. I had spent six weeks building that system — its rules, its costs, its history — and I wanted the reader to understand it before the story began. What I had actually done was write a textbook introduction to a class the reader never signed up for.

My editor, bless her, was kind about it. "I stopped caring by page two," she said. "Show me someone *using* it. Show me what it costs them."

She was right. I cut all four pages. The system is still there — every rule intact — but now the reader learns it the way we learn anything real: by watching, inferring, and occasionally being surprised.

## The Rule I Now Live By

**Your world is not a document. It is a lived-in place.**

Real people do not explain their own culture to each other. A character raised in a kingdom where the dead can speak does not think *the dead can speak here, which is unusual compared to other kingdoms*. She just hears her grandmother's voice in the frost and finds it ordinary.

The reader has to catch up. That is not a flaw — it is an invitation.

## Techniques That Actually Work

**Casual reference over formal explanation.** A character might mutter "not even a Veilborn could track us here" without any surrounding context. The reader understands: *Veilborn track people. They are known for this. This place is unusually safe.* Three words of worldbuilding, zero sentences of exposition.

**Let characters be wrong.** Unreliable narrators are useful in fantasy because they model the reader's own uncertainty. If your protagonist misunderstands something about the magic, and later learns the truth, the reader is discovering it alongside her — not being lectured at.

**Use the senses before the explanations.** I always ask myself: what does this place *smell* like? What sounds does it make at night? A city that smells of copper and cold smoke tells the reader something immediate and true before a single rule has been established.

## What You Get to Keep

None of this means your detailed notes are wasted. They are structural — they hold the world together, keep it consistent, make the rules feel real even when you never state them directly.

The iceberg principle applies here as much as anywhere: what the reader sees is one-tenth of what exists. But that one-tenth has to be earned, not summarised.

Build the whole thing. Show them the tip. Trust them to feel the rest.
